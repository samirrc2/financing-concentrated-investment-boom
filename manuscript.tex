\begin{abstract}
Aggregate financing can differ from that facing the typical investment project when investment is
concentrated. Among the 500 largest U.S. public companies, incremental operating cash flow covers
118\% of the 2022--2025 investment increase at the five largest investors and 104\% at the top ten,
which account for 52.5\% of it, but $-7$\% across the remaining 217 firms. This rank--coverage
gradient is absent in 2014--2017 and 2018--2021, one of which was more concentrated at the top.
Evidence on externally financed marginal projects therefore need not characterise the
investment-weighted aggregate. This is an accounting decomposition, not a causal financing test.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Among the 500 largest U.S. public companies, incremental operating cash flow over 2022--2025 covers
118\% of the incremental capital expenditure of the five largest investors and 104\% of that of the
top ten, which account for 52.5\% of the positive increment, but $-7$\% across the remaining 217
firms with rising investment (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). Net debt rises correspondingly: those 217
firms took on \$280 billion of cumulative net new debt, against $-\$3$ billion for the top five. The
five---Alphabet, Amazon, Oracle, Meta and Microsoft, selected mechanically as those with the largest
absolute increase in real capital expenditure---carry 38.7\% of that increment on their own.

Such a gradient matters because financing evidence gathered at the project margin need not
characterise the investment-weighted aggregate. Recent work documents the artificial-intelligence
buildout being financed through private credit, infrastructure funds, off-balance-sheet vehicles and
hardware-collateralised lending, and asks what the exposures imply for financial stability (Van
Nieuwerburgh, 2026; Thelisson, 2026; Ledesma Padilla, 2026). That evidence describes particular
projects and particular firms. Because an aggregate is a capital-expenditure-weighted sum, it is
dominated by wherever the incremental spending occurred, and here the incremental spending is
extraordinarily concentrated: the Herfindahl index of capital expenditure within the 500 rises from
173 in 2021 to 309 in 2025, while excluding the five largest spenders it moves only from 95 to 104.

Concentration alone does not produce the gradient. Repeating the same calculation over 2018--2021
and 2014--2017 gives coverage that is flat or rising across the distribution, with the tail the most
internally covered group in both, and the 2018--2021 window carries a \emph{higher} top-five share of
the increase than 2022--2025. What distinguishes the current episode is not that investment became
concentrated but that it concentrated among firms whose internal cash generation was unusually
strong relative to their own investment.

That corporate investment is concentrated, and that internal funds and investment covary at the firm
level, are both established (Grullon et al., 2018; Gutierrez and Philippon, 2017; Fazzari et al.,
1988; Kaplan and Zingales, 1997; Myers and Majluf, 1984). Our contribution is to document the
alignment between the two during this boom and to show it is historically unusual. Unlike Grullon
et al. (2018), which estimates how cash flow predicts investment, we report an ex-post coverage
ratio for one episode, and we interpret the exercise as an accounting decomposition of realised
flows, not a causal financing test.

\section{Data and methods}
Firm-level data are constructed from the SEC's XBRL \emph{frames} interface for 2013--2025 and
reconciled to the underlying filings; \ref{app:tags} documents the construction, which unions
alternative tags because a single-tag series would omit Amazon and 448 other filers, and
\ref{app:recon} the validation. We measure the buildout using total capital expenditure, following
Board of Governors (2026) and Van Nieuwerburgh (2026).

The universe is the 500 U.S. registrants with the largest fiscal-2024 revenue, combined revenue
\$19.75 trillion---the Fortune 500's ranking basis, excluding non-filers. Listed firms exclude a growing private segment (Doidge et al., 2017). Figures are deflated to
2025 dollars using the BLS producer price index for private capital equipment (WPUFD4131).

One measurement point bears on interpretation rather than construction: SEC fiscal-year labels are
not calendar years for off-calendar filers, so Oracle's 2025 observation extends through May 2026.
We retain the SEC convention.

The exercise cumulates incremental flows rather than comparing two annual
observations. For each series $X$ and firm $i$,
\begin{equation}
\Delta X_i \;=\; \sum_{t=2022}^{2025}\bigl(X_{it}-\bar{X}_i\bigr),
\label{eq:cum}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Coverage}_i \;=\; \frac{\Delta \mathrm{OCF}_i}{\Delta \mathrm{CAPEX}_i},
\label{eq:cov}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{X}_i$ is either the 2021 value (baseline A) or the 2017--2021 average (baseline B). The
ratio requires an observed baseline as well as a positive denominator: a firm absent in 2021 would
otherwise be assigned a baseline of zero, making its entire subsequent spending appear incremental.
Twenty-five firms fail that condition and are excluded, removing \$36 billion of spurious increment.
The condition holds for 267 firms;
firms whose investment fell are excluded rather than assigned a signed ratio. Because R\&D is an
operating expense already deducted in arriving at operating cash flow, it cannot enter as a
separately financed use and is reported as a memorandum item.

The five focal firms are selected mechanically from financial statements, not from AI disclosures.
Contemporaneous company filings independently identify AI-related compute capacity and data-centre
infrastructure as drivers of the investment acceleration for three of the five; \ref{app:ai} records
the evidence firm by firm, including the two cases where the filings do not support that reading.
Coverage above 100\% establishes that internally generated cash grew by more than investment did,
not that particular dollars financed particular assets. Every reported number is produced from
frozen, hashed source files by a single script, and the manuscript is gated against the resulting
claims file.

\section{Results}
\subsection{A historically unusual rank--coverage gradient}
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} reports the central result. Ranking the 267 firms with rising investment by
their cumulative incremental capital expenditure traces a rank--coverage gradient: internal cash
coverage peaks at the five largest investors and then falls at every successive cut, from 118\% for the top five to $-7$\% for the
remaining 217, while cumulative net new debt rises from $-\$3$ billion to \$280 billion. The top
ten firms account for 52.5\% of positive incremental capital expenditure at 104\% coverage.

Coverage is not monotone across the first three cuts: 67\% for the largest single investor and 70\%
for the top three, before rising to 118\% at the top five. This reflects the firm-level dispersion in
Section~\ref{sec:top}: Alphabet, Meta and Oracle lie between 63\% and 79\%, while Microsoft and
Amazon, ranked fourth and fifth, are at 122\% and 344\%. The decline is a property of expanding the
sample beyond the five largest investors, and that is the claim we make.

The gradient matters for any aggregate statement. Aggregate coverage is a capital-expenditure-weighted
average of firm-level coverage, so it is determined by where in the distribution the incremental
spending sits rather than by the typical firm. Firms with coverage below 50\% account for 30.4\% of
positive incremental capital expenditure.

The gradient is specific to this window. Table~\ref{tab:placebo} repeats the calculation over 2018--2021 and 2014--2017: coverage is flat or rising across the distribution, and the tail is the most internally
covered group in both. The 2018--2021 window carries a higher top-five share of the increase
(42.3\%) than 2022--2025 (38.7\%), so concentration alone does not produce the pattern.

The pattern is not a mechanical consequence of the five focal firms. Excluding them, 34.0\% of
positive incremental capital expenditure still occurred at firms with coverage at or above 100\%,
against 33.3\% including them. What the five contribute is weight at the top of the distribution,
not a different relationship between rank and coverage.

\subsection{The aggregate for the top of the distribution}\label{sec:top}
For the five largest incremental investors, cumulative incremental operating cash flow over
2022--2025 was \$438 billion against \$371 billion of cumulative incremental capital expenditure, a
coverage ratio of 118\%, with cumulative net new debt of $-\$3$ billion and net new equity of \$2
billion. On the 2017--2021 average baseline the corresponding figures are \$762 billion, \$588
billion, coverage 130\%, and net new debt \$37 billion. Both baselines give the same answer.

Coverage exceeds 80\% omitting any single firm, ranging from 81\% (omitting Amazon) to 136\%
(omitting Alphabet), and exceeds 100\% in four of the five cases. Firm-level coverage is nonetheless
dispersed---63\% at Oracle, 67\% at Alphabet, 79\% at Meta, 122\% at Microsoft and 344\% at
Amazon---so sufficiency is a property of the group aggregate and not of every constituent;
\ref{app:firm} reports the firm-level decomposition and the leave-one-out calculation.

\subsection{Attribution: AI-exposed firms identified independently}\label{sec:ai}
The rank--coverage gradient is constructed independently of AI labels; we therefore assess the AI
interpretation separately, using firms' contemporaneous filings and a measure built without
reference to any financing outcome. The firms at the bottom of the distribution are utilities,
airlines, pipelines and energy producers, whose borrowing is unrelated to compute. We score each registrant's most recent 10-K against a dictionary of AI
terminology fixed before any subsample result was computed, following established practice in
building firm-level measures from filing text (Loughran and McDonald, 2011; Hoberg and Phillips,
2016; Ante and Saggu, 2025); details are in \ref{app:dict}. The measure identifies the expected
firms without conditioning on industry, the highest scores being NVIDIA, Amazon, Meta, Advanced
Micro Devices, Microsoft, Oracle and Alphabet.

Within the top decile of AI intensity (27 firms) coverage is 128\% and cumulative net new debt is
$-\$24$ billion; at the top-quintile cutoff (53 firms) coverage is 138\% (Table~\ref{tab:ladder},
panel B). Coverage exceeds 100\% at every cut and net new debt is zero or negative throughout,
against $-7$\% and \$280 billion across the full distribution. The five largest investors account
for 92.5\% of the top decile's incremental capital expenditure, so this corroborates the main result
on an independently defined universe rather than testing it independently.

\subsection{The margin narrowed}
The result is bounded in time. Capital expenditure absorbed 31\% of the five firms' operating cash
flow in 2017 and 70\% in 2025, so internal financing slack narrowed sharply over the sample. That narrowing is
consistent with external financing becoming more important as the buildout progresses.

\section{Conclusion}
The financing of marginal projects need not characterise an investment boom in the aggregate. During
the AI buildout, internal cash coverage falls from 118\% for the five largest incremental investors
to $-7$\% for the remaining 217, while net debt rises down the distribution. The top ten account for
52.5\% of positive incremental capital expenditure at 104\% coverage. This rank--coverage gradient is
specific to the current boom: it is absent in the preceding two four-year windows, including
2018--2021, when investment was more concentrated at the top.

This reconciles, rather than contradicts, evidence on external financing: within public firms,
borrowing is concentrated away from the largest investors, while project-finance structures lie
largely outside our sample. The result is
time-specific; capital expenditure absorbed 70\% of the five firms' operating cash flow in 2025,
versus 31\% in 2017.

Two limitations bound the reading. This is an accounting decomposition of realised flows, not
evidence that particular dollars financed particular assets or a counterfactual of firm behaviour.
The sample covers large public filers and is silent on private firms and special-purpose
vehicles.

\section{AI-exposure measure: pre-specification and construction}\label{app:dict}
\textbf{Source.} The most recent 10-K for each sample firm, retrieved from SEC EDGAR via the
submissions API. HTML is stripped to text and lower-cased; 261 of the 267 sample firms yielded a
scoreable filing (six had no retrievable 10-K primary document).

\textbf{Pre-specification.} The dictionary and cutoffs below were fixed in the collection script
before any subsample financing result was computed, and the script header records this. No term was
added, removed or reweighted after inspecting a result.

\textbf{Dictionary.} Model and method vocabulary: \emph{artificial intelligence, machine learning,
generative ai, deep learning, neural network, large language model, foundation model, ai model, ai
capabilities, ai infrastructure, accelerated computing}. A separate infrastructure list (\emph{data
center/centre, graphics processing unit, gpu, compute capacity, accelerator, training cluster,
inference capacity}) is recorded but not used in the primary cut.

\textbf{Score.} $\text{AI intensity}_i = 10{,}000 \times (\text{dictionary occurrences}) /
(\text{total words})$. Distribution across the sample: median 0.64, 80th percentile 1.35, 90th
percentile 2.21.

\textbf{Cutoffs.} AI-exposed $=$ top decile (threshold 2.21, $n=27$); robustness at top quintile
(threshold 1.35, $n=53$).

\textbf{Face validity.} The ten highest-scoring firms are NVIDIA, Amazon, Meta, Advanced Micro
Devices, Microsoft, Medline, Oracle, Alphabet, Target and Workday. Seven of the ten are compute or
platform firms. The measure also admits firms that discuss AI heavily without building
infrastructure---Medline, Target, Workday---which is a known cost of a term-frequency filter and is
not corrected ex post. Because these firms are small incremental investors, they carry little weight:
the five largest incremental investors account for 92.5\% of the top decile's incremental capital
expenditure.

Capital expenditure unions \tg{PaymentsToAcquirePropertyPlantAndEquipment} and
\tg{PaymentsToAcquireProductiveAssets}; operating cash flow unions
\tg{NetCashProvidedByUsedInOperatingActivities} with its continuing-operations variant;
repurchases and dividends likewise union their alternates. Single-tag construction omits Amazon and
448 other filers representing \$404 billion of 2025 capital expenditure. Deflator: BLS PPI
WPUFD4131. All source files are frozen and SHA-256 hashed; every number in the manuscript is
verified against a machine-generated claims file by an automated build gate.
