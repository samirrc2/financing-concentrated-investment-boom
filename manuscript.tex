\begin{abstract}
Financing conditions at the project margin need not characterise an investment boom when investment
is concentrated. Among the 500 largest U.S. public companies, incremental operating cash flow covers
117\% of the 2022--2025 investment increase at the five largest investors and 102\% at the top ten,
which account for 52.8\% of it, but $-61$\% across the remaining 213 firms. The pattern is not a mechanical consequence of concentration: the more concentrated 2018--2021
window instead has the tail as its best-covered group. For firms making more than half the increase,
incremental internal cash was itself sufficient to cover it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Among the 500 largest U.S. public companies, incremental operating cash flow over 2022--2025 covers
117\% of the incremental capital expenditure of the five largest investors and 102\% of that of the
top ten, which account for 52.8\% of the positive increment, but $-61$\% across the remaining 213
firms with rising investment (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). The
five---Alphabet, Amazon, Oracle, Meta and Microsoft, selected mechanically as those with the largest
absolute increase in real capital expenditure---carry 38.9\% of that increment on their own.

Such a gradient matters because financing evidence gathered at the project margin need not
characterise the investment-weighted aggregate. Recent work documents the artificial-intelligence
buildout being financed through private credit, infrastructure funds, off-balance-sheet vehicles and
hardware-collateralised lending, and asks what the exposures imply for financial stability (Van
Nieuwerburgh, 2026). That evidence describes particular
projects and particular firms. Because an aggregate is a capital-expenditure-weighted sum, it is
dominated by wherever the incremental spending occurred, and here the incremental spending is
extraordinarily concentrated: the Herfindahl index of capital expenditure within the 500 rises from
173 in 2021 to 309 in 2025, while excluding the five largest spenders it moves only from 95 to 104.
That a few firms can dominate an aggregate is the granular mechanism of Gabaix (2011), Carvalho and
Gabaix (2013) and di Giovanni et al. (2014); we apply it to financing rather than to fluctuations.

Investment concentration and firm-level covariation between internal funds and investment are both
established (Grullon et al., 2018; Guti\'errez and Philippon, 2017; Fazzari et al., 1988; Kaplan and
Zingales, 1997; Myers and Majluf, 1984). We document the alignment between them during this boom and
show it is historically unusual, as an accounting decomposition of realised flows rather than a
causal test.

\section{Data and methods}
Firm-level data are constructed from the SEC's XBRL \emph{frames} interface for 2013--2025 and
reconciled to the underlying filings. The construction unions alternative tags: filers do not use a
single tag for a given concept, and a single-tag series would omit Amazon, the largest U.S. capital
spender, together with 454 other firms accounting for \$404 billion of 2025 capital expenditure
(\ref{app:supp}, Table~A2). We measure the buildout using total capital expenditure, following Soto et al. (2026) and Van Nieuwerburgh (2026).

The universe is the 500 U.S. registrants with the largest fiscal-2024 revenue, excluding
non-filers. The universe excludes private firms by construction. Figures are deflated to
2025 dollars using the BLS producer price index for private capital equipment (WPUFD41312).

The exercise cumulates incremental flows rather than comparing two annual
observations. For each series $X$ and firm $i$,
\begin{equation}
\Delta X_i \;=\; \sum_{t=2022}^{2025}\bigl(X_{it}-\bar{X}_i\bigr),
\label{eq:cum}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Coverage}_i \;=\; \frac{\Delta \mathrm{OCF}_i}{\Delta \mathrm{CAPEX}_i},
\label{eq:cov}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{X}_i$ is either the 2021 value (baseline A) or the 2017--2021 average (baseline B).

Let $s_i = \Delta\mathrm{CAPEX}_i / \sum_j \Delta\mathrm{CAPEX}_j$ be firm $i$'s share of the
aggregate increment. Then
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Coverage}_G \;=\; \sum_i s_i\,\mathrm{Coverage}_i ,
\label{eq:agg}
\end{equation}
so an aggregate coverage ratio is the increment-weighted mean of firm-level coverage. A group's
aggregate therefore places greatest weight on its largest incremental investors, whatever the
coverage of the remaining firms in it.

The ratio requires an observed baseline as well as a positive denominator: a firm absent in 2021 would
otherwise be assigned a baseline of zero, making its entire subsequent spending appear incremental.
Of the 500, 404 report capital expenditure at both endpoints: 263 have a positive cumulative
increment and 141 do not. The remaining 96 lack an observation at one or both endpoints, 17 of them
reporting 2025 spending with no 2021 baseline, which would otherwise enter as \$28 billion of
spurious increment. Firms whose investment fell are excluded rather than assigned a signed ratio.

The five focal firms are selected mechanically from financial statements, not from AI disclosures;
\ref{app:supp}, Table~A1, records what each firm's own filings say, including cases where the
capex--AI attribution is only partial. Coverage above 100\% establishes that internally generated cash grew by more than investment did,
not that particular dollars financed particular assets. Every reported number is produced from
frozen, hashed source files by a single script, and the manuscript is gated against the resulting
claims file.

\section{Results}
\subsection{A historically unusual rank--coverage gradient}
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} reports the central result. Ranking the 263 firms with rising investment by
their cumulative incremental capital expenditure traces a rank--coverage gradient: internal cash
coverage peaks at the five largest investors and then falls at every successive cut, from 117\% for
the top five to $-61$\% for the remaining 213. The top ten firms account for 52.8\% of positive
incremental capital expenditure at 102\% coverage. The top-five figure is not one firm's doing:
coverage remains at least 79\% omitting any single one of them.

The positive-increment restriction affects only the tail. Coverage at every cut through the top
fifty is invariant to it, because those cuts contain the same firms either way; admitting the 141
firms whose investment fell, with signed increments, raises the top-five share of the increment from
38.9\% to 46.0\% and leaves the gradient steeper, not weaker.

Concentration and internal financing are not the same phenomenon. The 2018--2021 window was more
concentrated at the top than 2022--2025---a top-five share of 41.9\% against 38.9\%---yet its rank pattern runs the other way: the tail is its best-covered group, as it is in 2014--2017
(Table~\ref{tab:placebo}). Concentrated investment booms are not internally financed booms as a
rule. What is unusual about the present one is that the two coincide.

Three checks bound the top-five aggregate. First, the baseline: on the 2017--2021 average the corresponding
figures are \$757 billion, \$587 billion and coverage 129\%, so both baselines give the same answer.
Second, dependence on one firm: coverage ranges from 79\% (omitting Amazon) to 135\% (omitting Alphabet) and exceeds 100\% in four of the five cases. Third, dispersion within the group:
firm-level coverage runs from 63\% at Alphabet and 64\% at Oracle to 118\% at Microsoft and 361\% at
Amazon, which is why the cumulative ratio is not monotone across the first three cuts---63\%, then
68\%, then 117\%---and why sufficiency is a property of the group aggregate and not of every
constituent (\ref{app:supp}, Table~A3). Coverage is also an end-of-period property: cumulated from
the 2021 baseline it is $-39$\% through 2022 and reaches 117\% only in 2025
(Figure~\ref{fig:main}c).

\subsection{The gradient survives sector exclusions}\label{sec:comp}
One concern is that the tail contains regulated, extractive and financial firms whose cash-flow
economics differ from the rest of the sample. Excluding these groups does not attenuate the
gradient. The 25 regulated
and extractive firms in the tail are its best-covered part, at 122\% coverage on 19.9\% of tail
investment, and the 25 financial firms cover 298\%. The tail's $-61$\% comes from the other 163
firms, which carry 73.0\% of tail investment at $-146$\% coverage. Dropping regulated, extractive
and financial firms from the sample altogether leaves the gradient intact and steeper: 117\% at the
top five, 97\% at the top ten, 80\% at the top fifty and $-324$\% across the remainder
(\ref{app:supp}, Table~A3). An AI-intensity cut built from 10-K text without reference to any
financing outcome gives the same answer (\ref{app:supp}, Table~A1).

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:lim}
Three bound the reading. (i) The sample selects on an observed 2021 baseline and on a
positive increment; Section~3.1 reports what relaxing the second does. (ii) Coverage is an accounting relation between realised flows, not a financing
statement: it does not trace particular dollars to particular assets and identifies no
counterfactual of firm behaviour. (iii) The universe is large public filers, so private firms and
special-purpose vehicles lie outside it by construction.

\section{Conclusion}
The financing of marginal projects need not characterise an investment boom in the aggregate: during
the AI buildout, internal cash coverage falls from 117\% at the five largest incremental investors
to $-61$\% across the remaining 213. The gradient is specific to this boom, absent in the preceding
two four-year windows including the more concentrated 2018--2021.

This reconciles, rather than contradicts, evidence on external financing: project-finance structures
lie largely outside our sample. For the firms making more than half the increment, incremental
internal cash was in aggregate sufficient to cover their part of the boom, so the headline size of
the investment boom overstates the amount of incremental investment for which incremental internal
cash was insufficient. The result is time-specific; capital expenditure absorbed 70\% of the
five firms' operating cash flow in 2025 against 31\% in 2017, so internal financing slack narrowed
sharply over the sample.
